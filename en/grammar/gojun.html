Chapter 1Word Order

Ruwish is an isolating language: words never change their form, and there are no particles or endings that mark a word’s role in the sentence, such as subject or object. That makes the order in which words are placed the most important clue to a sentence’s meaning.

This page explains what goes where in a Ruwish sentence.

1. The framework of a sentence

A Ruwish sentence follows the order subject → verb → object (SVO).

我I
食eat
檎果apple

“I eat an apple.”

What follows the verb depends on the verb. Ruwish sentences fall into the following four patterns.

① Subject + verb

A sentence in which nothing follows the verb.

我I
歩walk

“I walk.”

② Subject + verb + object

A sentence in which the verb is followed by the target of the action (the object).

我I
食eat
檎果apple

“I eat an apple.”

③ Subject + verb + complement

A sentence that describes a quality or state of the subject. 很 is a copular verb. It is followed by an adjective and shows what state the subject is in.

斯this
檎果apple
很(copular verb)
大big

“This apple is big.”

④ Subject + verb + object + complement

A sentence meaning “the subject brings the object into the state of the complement”.

Sentences about handing something to someone, such as “give someone something” (giving sentences), are treated in many languages as sentences with two objects (“her” and “an apple”).

Ruwish, however, does not set up a separate pattern with two objects. Instead, one of them is treated as a complement. Ruwish expresses a wide range of things with pattern ④, so it is easier to explain giving sentences as one of its uses (more on this in a later chapter).

我I
給give
檎果apple
他女she

“I give an apple to her.”

This sentence means “the ownership of the apple (object) becomes hers (complement)”, and is loosely translated as “I give an apple to her”. The receiver is a complement, not an object, so “apple” comes first and “her” comes after.

2. Words attached to nouns

Adjectives

Adjectives are placed before the noun.

我I
食eat
大big
檎果apple

“I eat a big apple.”

Possession

To show the owner, place 的 between the owner and the noun.

私們we
的of
家house

“our house”

When it is clear who the owner is, 的 may be left out.

他男he
妻wife
毎日every day
食eat
檎果apple

“His wife eats an apple every day.”

Numbers

Numbers are also placed before the noun.

三three
檎果apple
在exist

“There are three apples.”

3. Words attached to verbs

Adverbs that modify a verb, and auxiliary verbs, are placed directly before the verb.

我I
過(past)
食eat
檎果apple

“I ate an apple.”

我I
可can
食eat
橘果mandarin orange

“I can eat a mandarin orange.”

4. Where other words go

Words of time are placed after the subject. (“His wife eats an apple every day” in section 2 has the same form.)

他he
先日yesterday
食eat
檎果apple

“He ate an apple yesterday.”

To ask a yes-or-no question, place 嗎 at the end of the sentence.

尓you
食eat
橘果mandarin orange
嗎(question)

“Do you eat mandarin oranges?”
